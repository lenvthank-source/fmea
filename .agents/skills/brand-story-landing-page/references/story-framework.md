## Source and limits

This framework adapts TED-Ed's *Public Speaking 101*, episode 10, "How to present your ideas effectively." It was built from the lesson page (summary, vocabulary and key lines), not a full transcript. If the user can paste the transcript or notes, compare against it and adjust the mappings below. The lesson is about stage talks; the mapping to web pages is an adaptation and should be described that way.

## The core idea: revelation

The lesson's central move is **revelation**: the act of making something previously unseen or unknown visible to an audience, in a way that delights and inspires. Sharing an idea well means getting the audience to picture it, wonder about it, and get curious, not just receive facts.

On a page, this changes the question you ask for every section. Not "what do we need to say?" but "what is the visitor about to see for the first time, and how do we let them see it?"

## Three reveal types and their page sections

### 1. Journey: walk the visitor through the work

The lesson's picture is a guided studio tour or a hike with an explorer: don't just present the result, take people along the path, including the false starts, so they get curious and see how the thing came to be. Its example is an illustrator who showed drawings together with mistakes and dead ends.

Page sections that apply it:
- Origin or "why this exists" moment, ideally with a real photo, document or object
- Process strip: materials, steps, tools, people at work (photos, short loops, annotated diagrams)
- Honest detail: what was tried and abandoned, what changed the design
- Scroll as the walk: each screen is a stop on the tour

Best for: craft, food, hardware, studios, services where how it's made is the value.

### 2. Demonstration: show it working

The lesson says a demonstration aims to amaze and open a sense of possibility, and that for something new the audience wants to see it in action. Its typical arc: an initial **tease**, an **explanation or context**, the **demo itself** (visual and dramatic), then the **significance**, meaning the impact it could have.

Page sections that apply it:
- Hero as tease: a striking visual or question, with the full explanation held back
- Context: the problem or situation, briefly, in the visitor's terms
- Demo: the product doing its thing. Use real screen recording or video when available, otherwise an animated diagram or interactive moment that shows cause and effect
- Significance: what changes for the visitor because of this (outcomes, before/after)

Best for: software, devices, new inventions, anything with a mechanism that can be shown.

### 3. Vision: make the future theirs

The lesson says a vision of the future should be so vivid and compelling that the audience adopts it as their own. Its example is an architect showing one clear image of a place many people would want to live in.

Page sections that apply it:
- A closing (or opening) section built around one vivid image or scene of the world after the brand succeeds
- Language in the present tense about that world, from the visitor's point of view
- A call to action that reads as stepping into that future, not "sign up"

Best for: mission-led brands, movements, platforms, products whose value is a changed way of living or working.

## Choosing and combining

The lesson says to select, combine and adjust types to serve the idea. Use these hints from the intake:

| If the brand's strongest asset is... | Lead with | Often add |
|---|---|---|
| A mechanism you can show working | Demonstration | Vision as the close |
| A making process, craft or founder story | Journey | Vision as the close |
| A changed future or a cause | Vision | Demonstration to make it believable |
| A new category the visitor must grasp | Demonstration | Journey for trust |

Rules of thumb:
- One lead type sets the page's shape; the others support it in one or two sections each.
- Not every page needs all three. A page forcing all three can feel long and padded.
- Whichever type leads, the hero should still be a tease and the page should end on significance or vision, so the visitor leaves knowing why it matters.

## Writing the justification

The user wants the page to convey *and justify* the ideas. For each section, state:

**Section → what the visitor sees for the first time → principle applied.**

Examples of the form (adapt, don't copy):
- Hero → a half-revealed result that raises a question → tease, so curiosity starts before explanation
- Demo band → the product running on a real input → demonstration, so possibility is seen not claimed
- Process strip → early prototypes next to the final → journey, because dead ends build trust and interest
- Closing scene → a day in the customer's changed life → vision, so the visitor adopts it as theirs

Keep the language plain and short, and don't quote the lesson at length; paraphrase.
